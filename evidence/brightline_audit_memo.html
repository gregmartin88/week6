SECTION 6

Disclosure timing: harm reduction vs. the duty of candor

The conflict: a consequentialist case can be made for waiting until the facts are complete and a fix is ready, to avoid panic, inaccuracy and harm to the business. The duty of candor says customers misled by false statements are owed the truth now. Each option below is scored on both.

OptionWhat it meansHarm reductionCandorVerdict
A · Full and immediatePublish every allegation to all customers and the public this week, before testing.Mixed Stops reliance fast. But unverified items (e.g., the HBCU pattern) could be overstated. Customers may switch to manual screening that is also biased and unmonitored.Strong Though disclosing claims as fact before they are verified is a candor failure of its own.Rejected
B · Fix, then tellDisclose after investigation and remediation, roughly 90 days.Poor Every screening day under v3.3 risks unlawful rejections of older and other affected applicants. Those people have no voice in the delay. The avoided harms are mostly to Brightline.Fails False claims D-01 to D-04 keep working on customers for 90 more days. Delay lets the company profit from the deception.Rejected
C · Only complainantsTell the 5 customers who raised issues; stay silent with the other 295.Poor The disparity is in the model, so it affects all deployers.Fails Selective disclosure rewards customers who noticed and leaves the rest misled.Rejected
D · Staged, verified facts firstStop false claims at once. Within 10 business days, tell all customers the facts proven by documents, with interim safeguards. Disclose further findings as they are verified.Strong Customers get usable mitigations along with the bad news, which limits panic and protects candidates fastest.Strong Everything stated is verified. Nothing known is held back. Open items are labeled "reported, under test" rather than hidden.Recommended
Why D holds upReasoning
The conflict is narrower than it looksHarm reduction, measured on candidates rather than the company, also favors speed. The only real harm-reduction case for delay is accuracy. Option D answers it by limiting the first notice to facts the documents already prove: the stale card, the failed v3.2 gate, the untested v3.3 and the false sales claims. None of these needs more investigation.
Candor sets a floor; consequences set the shapeStopping false claims and correcting them is not up for negotiation, because the deception is still running. The timing and packaging of everything beyond that floor (safeguards, staging, rollback) is where harm reduction rightly governs.
Why 10 business daysIt matches the charter's own triage standard (P7), so Brightline is held to its own promise. It is long enough for outside counsel to review the notice and for an emergency re-test of v3.3, and short enough that the company cannot keep profiting from the deception for a whole quarter.
Who decidesThe Audit Committee, not management. The managers named in Section 5 have a conflict of interest over timing.

Recommended sequence

  1. Day 0–2: Withdraw the "Bias/Risk v4" template and all "bias-free" collateral. Freeze override authority on release blockers. Confirm protection for the whistleblower and both CSMs.
  2. Day 0–5: Emergency independent AIR test of v3.3. Decide whether to roll back to v3.1, the last version that passed. Get outside counsel's view on regulatory notice obligations.
  3. By day 10: Notice to all 300 customers: corrected claims, the v3.2 age finding, v3.3 test status, and interim safeguards (suppress graduation year, human review before rejection, run their own AIR check, re-check LL144 postings).
  4. Day 10–45: Publish an updated model card. Follow-up notice with verified results on the open items (gap penalty, HBCU, non-native speakers).
  5. Ongoing: Quarterly board meetings reinstated, with an Audit Committee member attending. Semi-annual reports actually delivered.
